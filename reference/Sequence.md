# Sequence

The order of events, plainly. Companion to Timeline.1 and Timeline.1-Explained.

---

## Four corrections to the intuitive version

The natural way to tell this story is: *the ice cracked, that made a wave, the wave broke the other shelves, and they fell into the sea.* Three parts of that are wrong.

### 1. Shelves, not sheets

An **ice sheet** is the grounded mass sitting on the continent, bearing its weight on bedrock. There are two — East Antarctic and West Antarctic.

An **ice shelf** is the floating extension where a sheet reaches the sea and lifts off the bed.

Your four collapsing bodies are **shelves**: Amery, Ross, Filchner-Ronne, and the Amundsen shelves (Thwaites and Pine Island). Keep the two words distinct throughout the book. Almost every piece of physics here turns on which one is meant.

### 2. The shelves are already in the sea

A shelf floats. It does not fall into the water — it disintegrates in place, converting from a rigid slab into **mélange** over minutes to weeks.

This is exactly why the first reading says *no tsunami*. Floating ice has already displaced its own weight of water, so removing it changes sea level not at all, and displaces nothing that could make a wave. That reasoning is correct, and it is the reasoning that cancels the warning.

### 3. The first detection comes before the cascade, not after

Cape Leeuwin hears shelf one at **T+52 minutes**. The other three shelves do not fail until **six to ten hours later**.

So Vienna is already processing a single anomalous event while the second, third and fourth collapses are still hours in the future. Nobody is looking at four pins on a map on the first morning. There is one pin, and it looks like a large iceberg.

### 4. **The structural error: one wave cannot do both jobs**

This is the correction that matters.

**Wave A — the circumpolar wave.** Short wavelength, a few kilometres. It disperses (its long components outrun its short ones, and the packet frays), so it dies inside the Southern Ocean. It is entirely capable of finishing a shelf already at breaking point, because ice fails in **flexure** and flexure needs the right *period*, not great height. It is not remotely capable of reaching Mumbai.

**Wave B — the sediment wave.** Each shelf collapse dumps a sudden load change onto the continental slope beneath it, where thousands of cubic kilometres of glacially-ground sediment sit at their angle of repose. Water has to seep through that mud before the **pore pressure** between grains re-equilibrates, and seepage through fine sediment is slow. The slope therefore fails **hours later**. When it goes it runs out hundreds of kilometres, and a source that travels that far radiates a two-hundred-kilometre wave that crosses an ocean without dispersing.

**Two collapses, two clocks.** The ice makes the trigger. The mud makes the tsunami.

### And one omission

Sea level rise appears nowhere in the intuitive version, because it has no wave in it. It comes from the **grounded** ice behind the vanished shelves accelerating once its buttressing is gone — months to decades. That is the third act, and it is the part that never ends.

## The corrected sequence

Two tracks — **ICE** and **MUD** — running on different clocks, plus a third, **SEA LEVEL**, that opens later and never closes.

---

### T+0 · ICE
**Amery shelf fails.** A rift reaches critical length. The fracture front runs 100 km in one to three minutes, limited by the Rayleigh wave speed in cold ice (~1,750 m/s ceiling; real cracks run 500–1,500 m/s). The shelf unjams into mélange.

*Nothing has entered the sea. Sea level is unchanged.*

### T+2–10 min · ICE
**Near-field wave.** Steep, chaotic, short-wavelength; hundreds of metres of runup inside Prydz Bay. Davis (AU), Zhongshan (CN), Progress (RU) and Bharati (IN) are destroyed.

*Four stations go silent together. It reads as a communications fault, and will for hours.*

### T+11 min · SIGNAL
**Seismic arrival**, worldwide. Long-period, no impulsive onset, no coherent P wave. Recognisably not an earthquake — and therefore not processable as one. No origin time, no location, no magnitude, no mechanism.

### T+52 min · SIGNAL
**Cape Leeuwin (HA01) hears it.** Broadband, 11→4 Hz over 340 s, back-azimuth 189°.

**This is the first observation.** At this moment exactly one shelf has failed.

### T+1–2 h · INSTITUTIONAL
Vienna issues its first automatic bulletin. One anomalous non-seismic event in Prydz Bay. It looks like a very large iceberg.

### T+3–5 h · SIGNAL
**Infrasound arrival** at distant stations (~340 m/s). Inaudible. Perceived, where it is perceived at all, as pressure and rattling glass — and in a patchy geography, heard at 200 km and not at 80, because sound bends back down from the stratosphere in rings.

### T+4 h onward · SIGNAL
**Barometric pulse** begins its transit at ~315 m/s. Recorded by weather stations, home barometers and smartphones worldwide.

### T+5–9 h · INSTITUTIONAL
**DART buoys report the first wave: under 4 cm, short period, visibly dispersing.**

**Warnings cancelled. Correctly.** Every sentence in that bulletin is true.

### T+6.6 h · ICE
**Ross front flexes and fails.** The circumpolar wave, travelling at √(gh) ≈ 713 km/h over 4,000 m, arrives and finishes a shelf already at criticality.

*It does not weaken the shelf. The shelf was already at the edge. The wave is the last flexure.*

### T+7.5 h · ICE
**Filchner-Ronne fails.**

### T+9.6 h · ICE
**Amundsen shelves fail.**

*All four shelves are now gone. Still nothing has happened to any city, and sea level has not moved.*

### T+11 h · **MUD — the track opens**
**Prydz Channel Fan fails.** Runs out 700–900 km. Fifteen to thirty minutes of long-period seismic signal, no impulsive onset, consistent with a sustained horizontal force rather than a slip.

A source translating that far radiates as a source hundreds of kilometres *wide* — long wavelength, non-dispersive, ocean-crossing.

### T+14–20 h · MUD
**Crary Fan** (Weddell), then the **Ross** and **Belgica/Pine Island** fans.

### T+16–22 h · INSTITUTIONAL
**DART sees the real one.** Period ~20 minutes, amplitude ~0.5 m in deep water, holding together.

*The interval between the cancellation and this moment is the whole first act.*

### D+1 · ARRIVALS

Landslide sources **beam** — energy radiates perpendicular to the direction the sediment moved, like a headlight rather than a bare bulb. Four fans, four beams, four basins.

**But not one day.** The distances differ by a factor of four, so arrivals spread across more than a full day, and warning time varies enormously from place to place. That asymmetry is free structure.

| Beam | Target | Distance | After its fan fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ross | New Zealand | ~3,000 km | ~4.5 h |
| Amundsen | Chilean coast | ~4,000 km | ~6 h |
| Crary | Río de la Plata | ~5,000 km | ~7 h |
| Prydz | Maldives | ~8,000 km | ~11 h |
| Amundsen | Galápagos | ~8,000 km | ~11.5 h |
| Ross | Hawai'i | ~10,000 km | ~14 h |
| Crary | Lisbon | ~13,000 km | ~19 h |
| Crary | US eastern seaboard | ~14,000 km | ~20 h |

New Zealand and Chile get almost nothing — four to six hours, and the fan failure is itself hard to interpret in that window. Lisbon gets most of a day. **The Galápagos, arriving around T+29 h overall, are hit after the Indian Ocean has already been destroyed — so they get real warning, and the scene there is an evacuation rather than an ambush.**

### The wave arrives

Not as a breaking wall. As a tide that rises for ten minutes and does not stop, withdraws far enough to strip a harbour to mud, and returns — for six hours, with the third or fourth surge often the largest.

### **CORRECTION — named cities**

An earlier note in this project said New York and the Caribbean would receive only centimetres. **That was true for a single Prydz Bay source and is wrong for the four-sector premise.** The Crary Fan fires straight up an Atlantic that is open from pole to pole. Superseded; use the list below.

**Atlantic beam (Crary), in arrival order:** Montevideo, Buenos Aires, Santos, Rio de Janeiro, Recife → Dakar, Abidjan, Accra, Lagos → **Lisbon, Cádiz**, Porto, Vigo, Brittany, Cornwall, the Irish south coast → then across to Miami, Charleston, Savannah, Hampton Roads, Long Island, Boston.

The US eastern seaboard has a wide shallow shelf, which amplifies. The West African coast is low-lying and densely settled.

**Pacific beam (Ross):** Christchurch, Dunedin, Wellington, the Chatham Islands → Fiji, Tonga, Samoa → **Hilo** → the Aleutians.

**Eastern Pacific beam (Amundsen):** Valparaíso, Concepción, Callao, **the Galápagos** → Acapulco, southern California.

**Indian Ocean beam (Prydz):** Maldives, Gujarat, Mumbai, Karachi, Gulf of Oman.

> **Lisbon is a gift.** The 1755 earthquake and tsunami destroyed the city and broke the Enlightenment's confidence in a benevolent universe — Voltaire wrote *Candide* about it. A second tsunami reaching the same waterfront 271 years later, from a cause no one in 1755 could have conceived of, is thematically almost too neat to pass up.

### The drum — why some places get hit far harder than the beam predicts

Three real mechanisms, all named, all documented.

**Seiching.** A bay or harbour has a natural period of oscillation. A tsunami whose period matches it sets up a standing wave that keeps sloshing for hours after the wave itself has passed. **Hilo Bay** is the textbook case, wrecked repeatedly by waves that were unremarkable elsewhere on the same island.

**Basin normal modes.** Whole ocean basins ring. After large tsunamis, tide gauges across the Pacific show energy persisting for days at basin-scale periods, long after any direct arrival has gone through.

**Bathymetric focusing and edge waves.** Ridges and seamount chains act as lenses. Tohoku's energy was focused by seafloor structure onto specific stretches of the California coast — **Crescent City** took damage far out of proportion to its neighbours, for exactly this reason. Separately, energy can be trapped as **edge waves** running *along* a continental shelf rather than into it, producing arrivals hours late at places the direct beam missed entirely.

So a heavy hit on a Pacific-shaped drum is fair. The drum has favourite notes, and they are not where the beam points.

### Case study — Galápagos (Amundsen beam, ~T+29 h)

Included because it is the best-documented small-island analogue and because the archipelago is full of people who are not from there.

**Precedent.** The 2011 Tohoku tsunami travelled 14,500 km in 16 hours to reach the islands. Heights were 3–4 m generally, with 6 m runup in a pocket beach on Isla Isabela. Puerto Ayora — the largest harbour — flooded, with damage to shoreline structures. Yours arrives from 8,000 km with a comparable or larger source: scale up.

**Protective factor.** These are deep-ocean volcanic islands rising steeply from the abyss. Almost no continental shelf means almost no shoaling amplification. Unlike the Maldives, they also have real elevation to evacuate to.

**What people notice, in order:**

1. **The warning.** Hours of it. Ecuador's naval oceanographic service issues it; the waterfront empties; flights are cancelled and tourists are stranded.
2. **The tortoises go uphill.** In 2011 the alert forced the evacuation of the giant tortoises from the Charles Darwin Station to the highlands — Lonesome George among them. Staff carrying century-old animals up a hill in trucks.
3. **The boats run for deep water**, which is correct — a tsunami is nothing in the abyss and lethal in a harbour. **The tourists on boats end up safer than the tourists on land**, experiencing the whole thing as a flat, strange afternoon offshore watching a horizon that does nothing.
4. **Academy Bay drains.** Exposed rock, stranded fish, the smell of seafloor that has not been dry in ten thousand years.
5. **No wall.** In 2011 a six-foot surge pushed 200 m into town. Police stations, banks, the supermarket, the hotels, the restaurants on the front. The Darwin Station's waterfront laboratory took water straight through it.
6. **The currents do the real damage.** Moored instruments recorded modest averages in 2011, but simulation showed instantaneous speeds far higher — which is what tears vessels off moorings.
7. **It repeats for hours.**
8. **The animals.** Sea turtle and marine iguana nests destroyed; a flamingo lagoon at Garrapatero Beach lost entirely. In a place whose whole meaning is its fauna, that lands differently than flooded shops.

**Two dice rolls.** In 2011 it arrived at high tide, compounding everything, and at about 5:30 pm — roughly an hour before nightfall. Choose your tide and choose your light.

### D+2 onward · SECOND ORDER

The passing waves are themselves loading events: a 200-km wave crossing a continental slope swings bottom pressure by metres of water head, for six hours, on sediment already at its angle of repose.

- **D+2–3** — Bengal Fan, 9,000 km from the source
- **D+5** — Makran subduction zone ruptures, Mw 8.2. Last went in 1945. The segment was late.
- **D+8** — Currituck slide, off Virginia. The North Atlantic finally has damage of its own.

### D+12 · **THE HINGE**

Tide gauges are back in service and **none of them have returned to predicted level.**

Wave residual decays. This does not.

*Everything before this point is a disaster — a shape with an end on it. Everything after is a load.*

### M+1 onward · **SEA LEVEL — the third track**

No wave in it at all.

> **The mélange is a dead end. It is not the source of the sea level rise.**
>
> Two separate populations of ice, two separate fates, and confusing them is the most common error in this material.
>
> **The shelf** was floating before it failed. It unjams into mélange, disperses as icebergs, drifts north and melts. It contributes **nothing** to sea level at any point in that journey, because it had already displaced its own weight before the first crack appeared.
>
> **The sheet** — the grounded ice, sitting on rock behind the grounding line — never becomes mélange. It flows *as ice*, in accelerated ice streams, seaward for decades.
>
> And the timing is counter-intuitive: **sea level rises at the instant grounded ice crosses the grounding line and floats**, not when it melts. The moment it lifts off the bed it displaces its weight and the number has already moved. Whether that iceberg then melts in one year or forty makes no difference at all.

The **grounded** ice behind the four vanished shelves has lost its buttressing. Ice streams accelerate two- to eight-fold within months — measured, not theorised, after Larsen B in 2002.

Sea level starts climbing and does not stop, because much of the West Antarctic bed slopes **downhill going inland**: retreat carries the grounding line into deeper water, where the ice is thicker and discharges faster, which drives more retreat. No configuration balances until the bed rises again, roughly 600 km in.

Three and a half to four and a half metres, over decades. There is never a morning when it has happened.

## The load — does it "turn up the Ring of Fire"?

Broadly yes, but the geography is wider than the phrase implies, and the ordering is worth getting right.

**The load does not discriminate.** Three and a half metres of sea level is roughly 33 kilopascals on every square metre of ocean floor on Earth — Atlantic passive margins exactly as much as Pacific trenches. Nothing about the mechanism favours the Pacific rim.

**What varies is how close each thing already was to failing.** Rank them by remaining margin of stability:

1. **Sediment slopes** — holding on by almost nothing. Most responsive by a wide margin.
2. **Mature faults near the end of their cycle** — small margin. Responsive.
3. **Everything else** — a trench is driven by plate-scale forces that dwarf 33 kPa. Barely touched.

The Ring of Fire features prominently because it contains the most mature faults, not because the load prefers it.

**One point in favour of "turn up":** during the decades while sea level is *rising*, the load keeps increasing. It is a sustained forward push through that window, not a single step, and only becomes a permanent static offset once the rise stops.

**The calibration that makes it credible:** Earth tides modulate crustal stress by one to ten kilopascals and demonstrably modulate earthquake rates. Your load is several times larger than a mechanism already known to work, applied everywhere at once, and never withdrawn.

It cannot *cause* an earthquake. It advances the clock on faults already near failure — and after ten thousand years of tectonic loading, a great many are.

### Cascadia specifically

**The wave as trigger — possible, honestly weak.** A 200-km wave crossing the Cascadia margin swings bottom pressure by tens of kilopascals for six hours. Larger than the tidal perturbation. But it is *transient*: tides get millions of cycles to catch a fault at the wrong moment, and the wave gets one afternoon. Also, the Ross beam points up the 180th meridian toward Kiribati and the Aleutians; Cascadia sits at 125°W, substantially off-axis, reached only by spreading and bathymetric focusing.

**The load as trigger — weaker per square metre, far stronger overall.** It never passes, and it acts on every mature fault on Earth simultaneously.

**Cascadia's own state:** last ruptured January 1700; recurrence estimates run roughly 250–500 years. At 326 years it is mature, not dramatically overdue. Legitimately loaded, honestly described.

> **Best use in the book:** have a character argue for the wave while the data quietly supports the load. The wave is the dramatic candidate and the load is the plausible one, and the argument between them is a better scene than either conclusion.

## The compressed version

> Ice fails → a modest wave rings the other three shelves down → each collapse loads the slope beneath it → hours later the mud fails → **the mud makes the ocean-crossing wave** → and separately, over decades, the unbuttressed ice raises the sea.

**Three things the ice does not do:** it does not raise sea level when it fails (it was floating), it does not make the wave that reaches cities (the mud does), and it does not finish (the sheet behind it is still draining).
