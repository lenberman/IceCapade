# Silver Spring — where Daniel works

Setting research for S2, S4, S6, S12, S14. Verified against current NOAA sources; see the links at the foot.

---

## 1. The chain

**Department of Commerce → NOAA → National Weather Service → the Tsunami Program.**

The NOAA Tsunami Program is administered by the **National Weather Service**, and the Tsunami Program Manager sits inside the NWS **Marine, Tropical, and Tsunami Services Branch**. The Program leverages three other NOAA line offices — OAR (research, including PMEL), NOS, and NESDIS (satellites) — which is why Daniel spends his life coordinating people who do not work for him.

**What the Program actually owns:** the DART buoy network, seismic station feeds, coastal detectors, forecast improvement, data management, international hazard assessment, and the **National Tsunami Hazard Mitigation Program** (NTHMP) — a standing partnership with FEMA, USGS, NSF and the coastal states and territories.

**What it does not own: warnings.** Those are issued by two operational centres, neither of them in Maryland:

- **Pacific Tsunami Warning Center**, Ford Island, Pearl Harbor. Daniel's posting in his thirties.
- **National Tsunami Warning Center**, 910 S. Felton St, Palmer, Alaska.

This is the load-bearing fact about him. **He is headquarters, not operations.** He used to sit on a floor where things happened in fifteen minutes; he now sits in a building where nothing happens in real time and the unit of work is the fiscal year. Nobody demoted him. He was promoted out of the only room he was ever any good in.

**The S4 concurrence is real machinery.** The US participates in the Indian Ocean warning system through UNESCO's IOC as a member state, with NOAA as the technical agency. A concurrence on an international bulletin routes through the programme office. It reaches Daniel because he is the deputy director, not because anyone thinks he is the right judge of it.

## 2. The building

**Silver Spring Metro Center**, four towers at Colesville Road and East-West Highway, a few steps from the Metro station. NOAA has been there since 1993. **NWS is in SSMC2, 1325 East-West Highway.** SSMC1 is 1335, SSMC3 is 1315. The towers face each other across a plaza.

Two details that are free:

**There is no water.** The national tsunami program is run from an office block above a Metro station in inland Maryland. The Chesapeake is tens of miles off; the nearest moving water is Sligo Creek. Ledger #3 — Daniel never gets wet — is not just a plot rule, it is his commute.

**The campus looks at itself.** Four towers around a plaza means the view from most offices is another tower of the same agency.

## 3. Grade — where he sits

**Deputy director of a programme at NWS HQ is GS-15**, the top rung of the General Schedule. His boss being SES is exactly right and is the point: **GS-15 is the ceiling for a person who never converted to executive.** It is the last rung on which you still do the work rather than manage the people who do it.

That is the professional statement of *his end of the counter.* He occupies the highest place available to someone who did not ask for a different kind of place.

It also underwrites the marriage arithmetic the manuscript established. A GS-15 in the DC locality is well paid and nowhere near a K Street health-policy lobbyist. **Ellen makes plenty** is not a slight — it is a pay table.

And it makes Ellen's job offers legible. American Life wanting a *high-level climate analyst* is the standard GS-15 exit: agency expertise, private money. She is not asking him to be ambitious in the abstract. She is pointing at the specific door everyone at his grade walks through, and he says *sure*.

## 4. The window

The real answer is that federal space consolidation over the last fifteen years broke up perimeter offices; enclosed offices went interior and daylight went to the open floor, with SES generally keeping private offices. So a GS-15 in a renovated federal tower is not guaranteed a window and often does not have one.

Three ways to play it.

**(a) He has a window, and it looks at SSMC3. — Recommended.**

The man who runs the national tsunami programme has a window, and through it he can see his own agency. No water, no sky worth the name, just the other tower and the plaza between them. This is not a loss story and it asks the reader for no sympathy; it is the ordinary absurdity of the actual site, and it needs no backstory to install. It also gives you something for him to look at in S4 and S6 that is not a screen.

**(b) He had one and lost it in a consolidation.**

Strong for the character — a man who occupies assigned places, reassigned, never mentioning it. The cost is that it is a *story*, and it invites pity for him on his first appearance, which Part One cannot afford. Available as half a line later if you want it: *the office had been somebody else's until two reorganisations ago.*

**(c) No window, interior office.**

Flattest and most typical. Accurate, and it gives you no prop at all.

I would take (a). If you ever want the sting, (b) can be added retroactively in one clause without changing anything.

## 5. One prop worth having

The Program supports the **DART buoy network** — deep-ocean pressure recorders reporting continuously.

A status display of buoy health, on a wall, in a corridor. Everything green. Nobody watching it, because watching is what Palmer and Ford Island are for, and this is a building where the buoys are a line item.

That is the institutional twin of Oscar, and the book gets the comparison for nothing. Nālani has one machine looking at the ocean because she told it to, six years ago, when her grants stopped. NOAA has an entire network reporting into a hallway.
