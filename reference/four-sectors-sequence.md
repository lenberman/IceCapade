Each margin carries a trough-mouth fan — glacially ground rock flour delivered to the shelf break across successive glacial cycles and resting at the steepest angle it will hold. All four are mapped. All four have failed before.

The runout figures are not errors. A source that translates several hundred kilometres radiates as a source several hundred kilometres wide, and produces a wave that does not disperse.

**The Atlantic is open from pole to pole.** The Crary event has no barrier between it and Ireland.

## 7. Memorandum — E. Sandoval to Directorate

**Subject: Why four.**

The four shelf failures are not independent, and the mechanism is not tectonic.

Ice shelves fail in flexure. Long-period ocean waves flex them — the Wilkins breakup in 2008 has been attributed to swell originating in the Sumatra event four years earlier and half a world away. Our four shelves were at or near criticality; the literature on all four says so and has said so for a decade.

The Southern Ocean is the only ocean that closes on itself. There is no continent in the way. A long wave leaves any Antarctic embayment and travels circumpolar at √(gh) — about 713 km/h over 4,000 m — and reaches every other Antarctic embayment on a schedule that can be calculated on one sheet of paper.

I calculated it. Prydz to Ross, 6.6 hours. Prydz to Weddell westbound, 7.5. Ross to Amundsen, 4.6.

**Observed intervals: 6.75, 6.4, 5.0.**

The first shelf rang the others down. The ocean was the messenger and the ocean is a closed loop, which means the sequence had no external trigger and needed none. One shelf was enough.

I note without recommendation that a fifth and sixth ring are still in the water.

## 8. NOTICE TO MARINERS AND ARCTIC STATIONS — Day 2

A wave radiating from a point on a sphere converges again at the point opposite. Convergence is geometric and can be substantial.

The antipode of Prydz Bay lies at 68°N 105°W, in the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. The antipode of the Ross event lies near 78°N 5°E, north of Svalbard. The Weddell antipode falls in the Laptev Sea. The Amundsen antipode falls in the Kara.

**All four Antarctic antipodes are in the Arctic Ocean.**

Convergence amplitudes are not modelled for these waters and no gauge network exists to record them. The Arctic has almost no coast in the sense this Notice means, and almost no one on it, and the practical consequence of the largest wave convergence in recorded history is expected to be the fracture of sea ice.

## 10. Day 12 — the observation

**GLOSS / global tide gauge network — analyst note**

Of 187 reporting stations, 141 have returned to service. The waves have been over for ten days.

**Mean level across the 141 has not returned to predicted at any station.** Residual ranges +3 to +9 cm depending on basin, and the trend across the ten days is positive at 122 of 141.

This is not wave residual. Wave residual decays. This is the water that was ice on Day 0, and it is still arriving, because the shelves that were holding the grounded ice back are gone and the ice behind them has begun to accelerate. Ice streams observed after the Larsen B collapse ran two to eight times faster within months and did not slow.

## 11. Month 2 — the second-order failures

**Bengal Fan**, D3. The largest sediment body on Earth, loaded past yield by the Prydz wave crossing its slope. A 200-km wave swings bottom pressure by metres of head, for six hours, on a fan already at its angle of repose. Runup on the Meghna estuary; the delta is flat for a hundred kilometres inland.

**Makran subduction zone**, D5, Mw 8.2. Last ruptured 1945. The segment was late. Attribution unavailable and will remain so.

**Currituck sector**, D8, off the Virginia–Carolina margin, ~100 km³. Mapped since the 1980s. Until this morning the North Atlantic had recorded nothing and had been covering the sequence as a Southern Hemisphere event.

**Hikurangi margin**, D19. **Nuuanu flank, Hawaii**, D26, small. **Storegga north lobe**, D31. **Goleta slide**, D44, minor.

Nothing on this list is unprecedented. Every one of these features has failed before, some of them repeatedly, and the recurrence intervals are in thousands of years. The observation of interest is not that any one of them failed. It is that they failed inside forty-four days after ten millennia of not failing, and that the only quantity that changed in that window is the weight of the ocean sitting on top of them.

Ocean tides modulate crustal stress by one to ten kilopascals and demonstrably modulate seismicity. Global sea level has risen 11 cm. Eleven centimetres is 1.1 kilopascals, applied to every square metre of every ocean floor on Earth, and not withdrawn.

We are inside a mechanism already known to work. We have simply never seen it applied everywhere at once and left in place.

## 12. Month 3 — the fingerprint

**Memorandum. Distribution restricted.**

The following has been in the glaciological literature since the 1970s and has not reached this building, and I am writing it down plainly because I have now heard three principals state the opposite in three separate rooms.

**Sea level rise from Antarctica is not uniform, and the Northern Hemisphere gets the most of it.**

An ice sheet is a mass. It pulls the ocean toward itself gravitationally, and the sea currently stands higher near Antarctica than it otherwise would. Remove the ice and that attraction goes. Water flows away from the source. Local sea level around Antarctica *falls*. The far field receives the global mean **plus a further 10 to 30 per cent**.

The maximum of that distribution sits in the North Atlantic and the North Pacific.

New York, Boston, Rotterdam, Hamburg, London, Shanghai, and Tokyo will each receive substantially more rise than the global average figure being briefed, and more than any Southern Hemisphere city that was actually struck by a wave.

The hemisphere that watched this on television is the hemisphere being loaded hardest. I would like this in the top-line and not in an annex.

## 14. Private, Month 7

We got it wrong in a particular order and I want the order written down, because the order is the only part of this anyone will be able to learn from.

First we thought there were four events. That lasted eleven hours.

Then we thought it was ice, and we were right, and *being right stopped us looking at the mud* — and the mud was the whole thing. The wave that drowned Buenos Aires had not been ice for eleven hours by the time it left the seafloor.

Then we thought it was a disaster, which is a shape with an end on it, so for two weeks every question in every room was *where next*, and the answer was everywhere and nobody could hold that, myself included.

Then the gauges didn't come down, and we understood it was a load. Then the load started setting off slopes and faults that had been quiet since before agriculture, and we understood the load was doing work.

Then — three months in, from a paper published before I was born — we understood that the places nothing had happened to were the places getting the most of it.

Six corrections. Every one of them available in the literature on Day 0. Not one of them wrong through anybody's stupidity; every single one was a correct inference from the piece we happened to be holding.

I don't think the failure was intelligence. I think we kept asking what had *happened*, and the honest sentence never had a past tense in it. Nothing happened. Something started, and the start was the loudest part, and we spent seven months mistaking volume for significance.

The gauges are up 19 cm. Last month it was 16.

I am going to go on writing the number down. It seems to be the job.
