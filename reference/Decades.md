# The governing idea

> **Slow forcing, sudden failure.**

The input is a smooth ramp. The output is a sequence of discrete, unpredictable, locally total disasters — each arriving at a place that was fine the day before, each triggered by a threshold nobody had identified in advance.

This is what the decades feel like from inside. Not water creeping up a wall. **A city that works, and works, and works, and then one Tuesday in November doesn't.**

Everything in this document is an elaboration of that sentence.

---

# First, a correction to the intuitive figure

The natural summary is *four metres over decades, within one lifetime.* The lifetime part is nearly right. The four metres is too fast.

**The physical ceiling is ice flux through the grounding line** — width × thickness × velocity. Even driving the West Antarctic ice streams to the fastest speeds ever observed anywhere on Earth, the four sectors together deliver something on the order of two to four centimetres of sea level per year. That is twenty to a hundred times the current rate and utterly catastrophic. It is not four metres in thirty years.

**A defensible fast-end curve:**

| Year | Rate | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| 1–10 | 2–4 cm/yr | **~0.3 m by Y10** |
| 10–30 | 3–6 cm/yr, with surges | **~1.3 m by Y30** |
| 30–60 | 4–8 cm/yr | **~3.0 m by Y60** |
| 60–120 | declining as accessible marine ice is exhausted | **~4.3 m by Y120** |

**So a person aged thirty at Year 0, living to eighty, sees roughly three metres.** Not all of it. Most of it. Their grandchildren get the rest.

**Flag for the book:** even this is the aggressive end, and it is contested. The published fast-end literature on Antarctic contribution turns on whether **marine ice cliff instability** operates — the idea that ice cliffs above a certain height collapse under their own weight, exposing a new cliff behind. Some modelling groups get metre-scale contributions this century from it; others argue it is unnecessary and unsupported. Your four-sector premise justifies the fast end, but a knowledgeable reader knows the argument exists. **Let a character concede it.** A scientist who says "the number I am giving you is the top of a range that other people think is wrong" is more credible than one who does not.

# TRACK ONE — The cascade continues

**Nothing about the first fortnight is a complete list.** Discrete, sudden, regionally catastrophic events continue for decades, and the reason they do not taper like an aftershock sequence is that **the load keeps increasing.** Slopes that were stable at 30 cm of rise are not stable at 2 m. New candidates become primed as fast as old ones are used up.

### CX-01 | Y0–1 | The remainder of the primed slopes
Hikurangi (D19), a Hawaiian flank (D26), a Storegga lobe (D31), Goleta (D44) — then continuing at a few large failures per year.

### CX-02 | Y1–10 | Passive margin fans
Every large river fan on Earth is a candidate. **Nile, Congo, Amazon, Niger, Indus, Mississippi.** Each failure is a regional tsunami with its own beam direction and its own dead.

### CX-03 | Y1–20 | Volcanic island flanks
**Cumbre Vieja (La Palma)**, the **Hilina slump** on Kīlauea's south flank, and the Canary and Cape Verde flanks generally. These are the sources that put waves into the North Atlantic and the Caribbean directly, which the Antarctic beams never could.

*Narrative use: La Palma is the tsunami the public already fears. Having it fire — years late, for a reason nobody predicted — pays off an expectation you did not have to build.*

### CX-04 | Y1–30 | Mature faults
Cascadia, Nankai, the Himalayan front, the North Anatolian, the southern San Andreas, the Alpine Fault. Not caused — advanced. Attribution never resolves and the argument runs for the length of the book.

### CX-05 | Y5–20 | Volcanic response — **see Track Five**
Decompression melting under unloaded crust. This is large enough, and structurally important enough, to have its own track. The visible eruptions are the least of it.

### CX-06 | Y10–50 | Declining but not stopping
Rarer, larger intervals. The point at which people stop connecting each event to the original cause, and start treating them as ordinary bad luck.

# TRACK TWO — The rate is a staircase

### SL-01 | Y1–5 | The dynamic surge
The shelves are gone and the streams behind them accelerate. Steepest sustained rate of the whole event.

### SL-02 | Y5+ | **Pinning points**

This is the mechanism that makes the curve jagged, and it is worth understanding because it destroys any possibility of planning.

A grounding line does not retreat across a smooth floor. It retreats across real topography, and it **snags** — on bedrock highs, on sediment ridges, on ice rises. The Ross Ice Shelf grounds on Roosevelt Island and Crary Ice Rise. Thwaites sat on an offshore ridge until recently.

At a pinning point the retreat **stalls**, sometimes for years. Contribution drops. Rate falls. People conclude it is slowing.

Then it unpins, and the grounding line jumps inland to the next obstacle in months.

**So the curve is: a decade at two centimetres a year, then eighteen months at fifteen.** A staircase, and nobody can predict which step is next, because the bed topography under two kilometres of ice is known only in patches.

### SL-03 | Y10–60 | The staircase in operation
Every stall generates a public argument that the crisis is over. Every surge generates a public argument that it was always going to be worse than stated. Neither is right and both recur on roughly a decadal cycle.

### SL-04 | Y60–120 | Exhaustion
Accessible marine-based ice runs out. Rate declines. Level does not.

# TRACK THREE — Threshold failures

**The most useful section for the book.** Sea level is a smooth number. Almost nothing that depends on it fails smoothly.

## TH-01 | The market fails first, and by decades

**This is the single most important entry in this document.**

Insurers stop writing coastal policies. Without insurance, mortgages become unavailable. Without mortgages, property becomes unsaleable. Values collapse, the tax base collapses, and the municipality can no longer fund the defences that might have kept the water out.

**None of this requires the water to arrive.** It requires only a credible forecast that it will. The financial threshold fires while the physical threshold is still thirty years away, and it fires *faster* than the physics because it is a belief cascade rather than a hydrological one.

*This is already visible in Florida and California, at rates of rise an order of magnitude below yours.*

**A place can become uninhabitable while remaining dry.**

## TH-02 | Storm surge return periods compress

Mean sea level does not destroy cities. **Storms do, and storms are sudden.**

Every storm now starts higher. A surge that was a hundred-year event becomes a ten-year event with half a metre of rise, and annual with a metre. The compression is non-linear because surge heights follow an extreme-value distribution — the tail is steep, so a small shift in the baseline produces a large shift in frequency.

**Places do not drown gradually. They get hit by an ordinary storm that would have been survivable at the old baseline.**

## TH-03 | Gravity drainage reverses

Storm drains and sewage outfalls work by gravity. Raise the sea and they stop draining — then run backwards.

**Sunny-day flooding**: water rising through storm drains into streets on a clear, windless day at high tide. Already routine in Miami at present-day rates. It arrives decades before any wave, it cannot be walled out, and it makes a city unpleasant long before it makes it uninhabitable.

## TH-04 | Groundwater rises from below

Underappreciated and excellent material. Rising sea pushes up the coastal water table. Basements flood, foundations undermine, buried infrastructure corrodes, and **buried waste sites mobilise** — all with no surface water anywhere.

A seawall does nothing about this. The water is already inside the perimeter, underneath.

## TH-05 | Aquifers salinate — the city loses its water, not its land

Saltwater intrusion moves inland through permeable coastal aquifers. Once salt reaches a wellfield, that wellfield is finished, and no engineering recovers it on a human timescale.

**Miami's Biscayne aquifer** is the standard example. So are the Nile, Mekong and Ganges-Brahmaputra deltas.

*A city 20 m above sea level, with no flooding risk whatsoever, can be abandoned because its water became undrinkable.*

## TH-06 | Elevation thresholds are binary

Each of these has a number, and crossing it is instantaneous:

| Asset | Failure mode |
|---|---|
| Metro systems | Water at one specific entrance elevation. Sandy put ~4 m into lower Manhattan. |
| Airports | Many majors sit at 1–5 m. Schiphol is already below sea level. |
| Container ports | Quay design elevation. When the apron floods at high tide, operations stop. |
| Coastal substations | One flood ends them, and they feed everything else. |
| Nuclear plants | Sited coastally for cooling water. Decommissioning takes decades; spent fuel needs cooling throughout. |
| Seawalls, levees, barriers | Hold completely until overtopped, then fail completely. Venice's MOSE barriers were designed for a specified range. |

## TH-07 | Deltas and atolls

**Deltas** carry the largest populations at the lowest elevations: Ganges-Brahmaputra, Mekong, Nile, Irrawaddy, Niger, Pearl. Tens of millions each, at one to three metres, on ground that is also *subsiding* because the sediment that used to replenish it is trapped behind upstream dams.

**Atoll states** — Maldives, Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands — have no ground above three metres at all. Their threshold is not flooding. It is the freshwater lens beneath each island going saline, which happens first and ends habitation while the land is still there.

## TH-08 | Ecosystems keep up, or don't

Mangroves, salt marsh and coral reefs can all track *slow* sea level rise by building upward. Each has a maximum rate.

Exceed it and they drown — and they were the coastal defence. **The protection fails at the same moment the threat increases**, which is the general shape of everything in this section.

# TRACK FOUR — The slow systems

### SY-01 | Y1–10 | Antarctic Bottom Water reduction
Freshwater capping the Southern Ocean suppresses the sinking that fills the deep ocean basins. Global overturning slows. Invisible, enormous, and almost absent from disaster fiction.

### SY-02 | Y2–20 | Viscous rebound
The mantle beneath West Antarctica is unusually soft, so rebound runs on decades rather than millennia. Bedrock rises. In principle this is a *negative* feedback — a rising bed can restabilise a grounding line — and whether it arrives fast enough to matter is a genuine open question and a good thing for two characters to disagree about.

### SY-03 | Y10+ | The fingerprint fully expressed
Sea level *falls* near Antarctica. The far field receives the global mean plus ten to thirty per cent, with the maximum in the North Atlantic and North Pacific.

**The hemisphere that watched the waves on television absorbs the largest permanent rise.**

### SY-04 | Y10–50 | Agriculture
Delta salinisation removes some of the most productive farmland on the planet. This is the mechanism by which a sea level event becomes a food event, and it operates on rice.

### SY-05 | Y20–100 | Managed retreat becomes ordinary
First unthinkable, then contested, then policy, then unremarkable. Roughly a generation per stage.

# TRACK FIVE — Antarctic volcanism, and the feedback nobody modelled

**VO-** prefix.

## Why this mechanism works here and nowhere else in the book

EastCoast.md §5 rejects increased Atlantic volcanism, and the rejection was on two grounds: 40 kPa of sea level is a thousandth of the Iceland unloading, and it *loads* the seafloor rather than unloading it, which is the wrong sign.

**Antarctica is the exact opposite case, and it is the Iceland configuration precisely.** Kilometres of ice removed from directly above the volcanoes — **megapascals** of unloading, applied locally, to a volcanic province that is already active.

Iceland's eruption rate rose roughly **thirtyfold** after the last deglaciation. That number is transferable here in a way it is not transferable anywhere else in this project.

## The province is enormous

The **West Antarctic Rift System** is an active continental rift, comparable in scale to the East African Rift.

A 2017 survey identified on the order of **138 volcanic cones beneath the West Antarctic ice sheet**, most of them previously unknown — making it one of the densest volcanic provinces on Earth, and one nobody could see.

**Mount Erebus** (Ross Island) is persistently active with a long-lived lava lake. **Deception Island** (South Shetlands) has erupted within living memory. **Mount Takahe** and the Marie Byrd Land volcanoes sit directly in the Amundsen sector you are unloading.

---

### VO-01 | Immediate | Crustal flexure
Unloading bends the crust and opens pathways for magma. Fastest of the three mechanisms; acts on the same clock as the elastic rebound in E-02.

### VO-02 | Y1–20 | Magma chamber depressurisation
A magma chamber is pressurised fluid held in place by the weight of everything above it. Remove the weight and **existing** magma becomes more likely to erupt — no new melt required. This is the middle clock, and the one that produces eruptions your characters can watch.

### VO-03 | Y20–200 | Decompression melting
Less pressure on the mantle means more of it melts. Slowest, largest, and the one responsible for the Iceland thirtyfold figure. Runs well past the end of the book.

---

## VO-04 | Y5–60 | **The feedback — the important entry**

Subglacial volcanism does not produce spectacle. It produces **meltwater at the ice–bed interface**, and meltwater is what lets ice slide.

Geothermal heat flow beneath a grounded ice sheet is a direct control on ice velocity. Thwaites in particular sits over anomalously high heat flow, and quantifying it is an active research question right now.

**The loop:**

> unloading → increased volcanism → increased basal melt → lubricated bed → faster ice discharge → **more unloading**

**Time constant: decades to a century.** Positive. Sitting underneath the entire third act.

> **This is better than eruptions as set-piece, because it is a reason the curve steepens that nobody built into the models.**
>
> Discovered years late, from the mismatch between projected and observed discharge. It explains why the retreat outruns every projection, and it means the SL- track's staircase (SL-02) has a second, slower driver climbing underneath the pinning-point mechanics.
>
> A character can find this in a residual — the part of the acceleration that the ice dynamics do not account for.

## VO-05 | Any time | Direct shelf melting
A subglacial eruption beneath a floating shelf melts it from below directly. Local, fast, and a way to lose a shelf that has nothing to do with waves.

## VO-06 | Any time | Jökulhlaup
Meltwater trapped beneath ice by an eruption, released catastrophically. **Iceland's characteristic hazard, and it would be Antarctica's too** — a flood emerging from under an ice sheet with no warning and no watercourse to follow.

Good for a station-scale disaster in the middle act: something that kills people at a research base, years after the waves, from a mechanism nobody at that base was monitoring.

---

## The caveats — have a character voice these

**Eruptions are common in the Antarctic record. Demonstrating the deglaciation link specifically is not settled.** The correlation is inferred from Iceland and from ice-core tephra, not established for Antarctica.

**Response time is poorly constrained.** Whether VO-02 takes five years or fifty is genuinely unknown.

**And rebound cuts the other way.** Isostatic uplift (SY-02) raises the bed, which can restabilise a grounding line — a *negative* feedback running simultaneously with VO-04's positive one. Which wins is an open question, and the West Antarctic mantle's unusually low viscosity means the rebound arrives fast enough to be in the race.

> Two feedbacks, opposite signs, similar time constants, neither well quantified. That is a genuine scientific fight for two characters to have, and it does not resolve inside the book.

# The epistemic arc, continued

Sandoval's understanding kept correcting itself through the first seven months. It keeps going.

**Y1–5 — "When does it stop?"**
Answer: it doesn't, and the honest phrasing is *we have rate, not time.* This is the period when the question is still being asked in that form.

**Y5–20 — The staircase.**
Every stall produces a claim that it is over. Every surge produces a claim that it was always understated. The scientific position — *we cannot predict the next step because we cannot see the bed* — satisfies nobody and is correct.

**Y10–30 — The market outruns the water.**
The realisation that places are being abandoned before they flood, and that no defence project can be financed once the belief has moved. Engineering stops being the constraint.

**Y20–50 — The handover.**
People who do not remember before. For them this is not a catastrophe, it is the coastline. The word for what happened stops being an event and becomes a period.

**Y50+ — The question changes.**
From *how do we stop this* to *what do we keep.* Which archives, which cities, which species, which graves. A different kind of argument entirely, and the one the book probably ends inside.
