## Your three questions, answered first

**Grounding line.** Close, but inverted. It is not where the shelf rests on rock — it is where the ice **stops** resting on rock and starts floating.

**Rift.** Right. A crack. See A-02 for the specific sense.

# PART A — PRECURSOR

## A-01 | Grounding line retreat and shelf thinning

Picture a glacier as extremely slow toothpaste flowing downhill toward the sea. Inland it rests on bedrock, and the ground carries its weight. As it approaches the coast the ice gets thinner and the seabed drops away, until the ice reaches a point where the water can hold it up. From there on the ice floats.

**The grounding line is that boundary.** Behind it, grounded ice sitting on rock. In front of it, a floating **ice shelf** — still one continuous body of ice, still attached at the back, but afloat.

The line is not a fixed feature. It moves. Melt the underside of the ice near the boundary and the ice gets thinner there; thinner ice floats sooner; the boundary shifts inland. That is **grounding line retreat**, and it is happening now at Thwaites.

This matters because of a distinction that runs through your whole book. **Floating ice has already displaced its own weight of water.** Melt it and sea level does not change — the same reason a glass of iced water does not overflow when the ice melts. Only ice sitting on *rock* raises sea level when it enters the sea. So the grounding line is the line between ice that counts and ice that does not.

**Thinning** is a separate measurement. Not just at the grounding line but across the whole shelf: how much thickness the ice is losing per year, mostly to melting from below by relatively warm ocean water circulating underneath. Satellites measure the *surface height*, and because the ice floats with a known fraction above water, surface height gives you total thickness.

Thinning matters because of **buttressing**, which is the single most important idea in this section. The floating shelf is jammed into a bay. Its sides scrape against the coastline and it snags on high points of the seafloor. That friction pushes back against the ice behind it. **The shelf is a cork.** It contributes nothing to sea level itself, but it holds back the grounded ice that does. A thinner cork grips less.

**How it is measured.** Two instruments recur throughout:

*Satellite altimetry* — a satellite fires a radar or laser pulse straight down, times the return, and computes the height of whatever it bounced off. Repeat for years and you get change.

*InSAR* — Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar. A radar satellite images the same ground twice from nearly the same position. Comparing the two images at the level of the radar wave's own phase reveals movement of the surface between passes, down to centimetres. It is used constantly here.

---

## A-02 | Rift propagation

A **rift** is a crack that goes all the way through the ice shelf, top surface to bottom. That is what separates it from a **crevasse**, which only penetrates part of the thickness. Crevasses are ordinary and everywhere. Rifts are structural.

Rifts open slowly — months to years — and grow at their tips as the ice around them stretches. When a rift reaches all the way across a shelf, or meets another rift, the piece in front separates and floats away. That is **calving**, and the piece is an iceberg.

**How it is measured.** Sentinel-1, a European radar satellite. The word **SAR** in its name means it builds images from radar rather than light, and that has a consequence you should use: **radar sees through cloud, and works in complete darkness.**

Antarctica spends months of the year in continuous night, under cloud. During austral winter, optical satellites — ordinary cameras — see nothing at all. Radar is the only eye. Any scene in your book where somebody is waiting for imagery, in winter, is waiting for a radar satellite to come back overhead on its orbital schedule, and that is a matter of days, not minutes.

---

## A-03 | Icequake swarm

Ice under stress cracks, and cracking makes noise. A single crack is an **icequake** — the same physics as an earthquake, in ice rather than rock, and much smaller.

In the weeks before a large calving event, the rate of icequakes climbs sharply. Thousands of small fractures, as the ice works itself toward failure.

The sound goes into the water and travels. A **hydrophone** — an underwater microphone — moored near the ice front picks it up. **Broadband** means the noise contains many frequencies at once rather than a single tone, which is characteristic of something breaking rather than something machine-made.

**This is a real, documented precedent and it is your opening chapter.** Before the Nansen Ice Shelf calved in 2016, moored hydrophones recorded hundreds of these signals for weeks. The calving became visible on satellite imagery much later. **The ice announced itself acoustically long before it announced itself optically** — and it did so to an instrument nobody was watching in real time.

---

## A-04 | Grounding zone GPS acceleration

This measures the ice moving, not the boundary moving.

Researchers plant GPS receivers directly on the ice surface. Each one reports its position continuously. Because the ice is flowing seaward, the receiver flows with it, and its record is a steady drift — a fast ice stream might carry it a metre or more per day, year in and year out.

That steady drift is normal and gets subtracted out. **What matters is departure from it.** If a receiver starts moving faster than its own established pattern, the ice under it has changed behaviour.

The numbers in the fragment — millimetres, then centimetres, then metres — are that *anomaly*, not the total motion. Millimetres of unexplained extra movement is a curiosity. Metres of it, in an hour, means the ice is no longer being held.

**Grounding zone** rather than grounding line, because the boundary is not sharp. Tides raise and lower the floating part twice a day, and the ice bends across a hinge a few kilometres wide. That flexing band is the grounding zone, and detecting the hinge in radar images is exactly how InSAR locates the grounding line at all.

---

## A-05 | Rate excursion, then loss of fix

The anomaly goes non-linear. Then the receiver stops reporting — either destroyed, or carried somewhere it cannot see satellites, or on a piece of ice that is no longer where the antenna expects the sky to be.

**Loss of fix** means the receiver can no longer determine its position. In the fragment, the telemetry simply ends.

# PART B — MINUTES

## B-01 | Fracture initiation

The moment a crack starts.

Any solid holds together until the force pulling it apart exceeds what its bonds can carry. That force per unit area is **stress**. Real materials always contain flaws, and stress concentrates at flaws, so failure begins at one and spreads.

Fracture initiation is the instant of transition. Before it, the ice is holding. After it, the ice is failing, and nothing anyone does will stop it.

Everything in Part A was the approach to this instant. Everything after it is consequence.

---

## B-02 | Fracture front crosses 100 kilometres

Once a crack exists, its tip runs. The question is how fast, and there is a hard physical ceiling.

A crack advances by breaking bonds at its tip. To break them, stress has to reach them — and stress travels through a solid as a wave, at a speed set by the material's stiffness and density. **The crack cannot outrun the news of its own existence.** The ice ahead of the tip has to be told it is being pulled apart, and that message arrives at wave speed.

The relevant limit is the **Rayleigh wave** speed — a wave that runs along a surface, slightly slower than the fastest waves inside the body. In cold glacial ice it is roughly 1,750 metres per second. Real cracks settle somewhere below that, typically 500 to 1,500 m/s.

**Do the arithmetic on your hundred kilometres.** At 500 m/s, 200 seconds. At 1,500, 67 seconds. **One to three minutes.**

So "at the speed of sound" in your draft is not poetic exaggeration for the crack — it is the correct order of magnitude, and it independently produces your "minutes." That is the rare case where the flourish and the arithmetic agree.

What it does *not* cover is the collapse behind the front. The fracture is sonic; the cascade of slabs toppling into each other is not. Larsen B took five weeks. **Two clocks, and the gap between them is where your characters live.**

---

## B-03 | Seismic arrival — the first signal to leave

Something this large shakes the ground, and the shaking spreads through the Earth as elastic waves. Instruments that record them are **seismometers**; the waves are **seismic**.

The fastest are **P waves** — P for primary, because they arrive first. They are compression waves, alternating squeeze and stretch travelling in the direction of motion, exactly like sound. Through crust and mantle they run at 6 to 8 kilometres per second.

**Compare the three speeds, because the comparison is your whole first act:**

| Path | Medium | Speed | Time over 4,660 km |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seismic | Rock | ~7,000 m/s | **11 minutes** |
| Hydroacoustic | Seawater | 1,485 m/s | **52 minutes** |
| Infrasound | Air | ~340 m/s | **~4 hours** |

One event. Three signals. Three arrival times hours apart. Three separate monitoring technologies, three separate bulletin streams, three sets of analysts — each characterizing its own signal correctly, none of them assembling the three.

---

## B-04 | Long-period seismic signature

This node explains why nobody treats the event as an earthquake, so it is worth getting straight.

**Period** is the time for one complete oscillation of a wave. Long period means slow. Short period means fast. Frequency is just period upside down.

An earthquake is a **sudden slip** — one side of a fault jerks past the other in seconds. A sudden event puts energy into short periods, and it produces a **sharp onset**: the trace is flat, then instantly it is not. Seismologists call that an **impulsive onset**, and the moment of the first P wave arrival is the single most important measurement in the field. From arrival times at many stations you triangulate the location.

Your event has none of that. The ice, and then the sediment, push on the Earth over *minutes*, not seconds. A slow push puts its energy into long periods — 20 to 200 seconds — and the trace shows no sharp beginning at all. Amplitude rises gradually over minutes and fades over tens of minutes.

**A seismologist can tell these apart instantly.** What they cannot do is process it. There is no impulsive onset to pick, so there is no origin time; no origin time means no location, no magnitude, no fault mechanism.

And every tsunami warning system on Earth begins by asking: where was the earthquake, how big, how deep, what mechanism. **Your event answers none of those questions, so the system has nothing to act on.** Not a failure. A category error built into the architecture.

---

## B-05 | Near-field wave

**Near field** means close to the source, where the wave has not yet organised into anything you would recognise as a tsunami. It is a collapse — a moving wall of broken water, far too steep to hold together, breaking continuously along its face, dragging churned ice and foam.

Steepness is what makes it lethal here and harmless later. **Runup** is how high water climbs the land, and near-field runup from a landslide can be extraordinary: Lituya Bay, Alaska, in 1958, recorded 524 metres from a rockslide a tiny fraction of this size.

Then it dies fast, for reasons in C-06.

---

## B-06 | Four stations lost

Davis (Australia), Zhongshan (China), Progress (Russia), Bharati (India) — four national research stations on one stretch of Prydz Bay shoreline. All four inside the near field.

**The first human datum is a silence.** Four stations stop transmitting within the same few minutes. That reads as a communications fault for hours, because communications faults in Antarctica are ordinary and four stations going quiet at once is not something anybody's alerting logic is built to interpret.

---

## B-07 | Mélange formation

**Mélange** is the correct word for what the shelf becomes: a dense floating mass of ice blocks, slabs and rubble, jammed together. The satellite images of Larsen B look like crushed blue glass poured into the sea.

The physics is **unjamming**, and the direction matters. A jammed granular material — sand, gravel, grain in a silo, ice blocks — holds rigid because its particles press against each other and cannot move without moving their neighbours. Unjam it and the particles slide freely. **A solid becomes a fluid without melting.**

This is why "gel" is precisely inverted in your draft. A gel is a *liquid* acquiring a solid network — fluid becoming rigid. You are describing the opposite. Jamming and unjamming genuinely are studied as phase transitions, so "phase transition" earns its keep. Only "gel" fights it.

---

## B-08 | Hydroacoustic arrival, Cape Leeuwin

The sound reaches Australia.

It travels in the **SOFAR channel** — a horizontal layer in the deep ocean, around 750 to 1,000 metres down, where the speed of sound reaches a minimum. Sound speed rises with temperature and rises with pressure; near the surface temperature dominates and speed falls with depth, while deeper down pressure takes over and speed rises again. Between the two sits a minimum.

Sound entering that layer is **trapped**. A ray straying upward bends back down, a ray straying downward bends back up. The energy never touches the seabed and never touches the surface, so it never scatters. It crosses an ocean basin almost intact. That is why eleven microphones monitor every ocean on Earth.

**Reading the bulletin line:**

- **0412Z** — Z is UTC. Everyone in this world works in UTC and nobody converts.
- **Broadband, 11→4 Hz** — many frequencies at once, sweeping downward. Both figures are below human hearing.
- **340 seconds** — nearly six minutes. An explosion lasts a fraction of a second.
- **Back-azimuth 189°** — the compass bearing the sound *came from*. Three hydrophones a short distance apart give bearing from the tiny differences in arrival time between them. One station gives a line, not a point.
- **Origin ~0320Z** — computed backwards: 4,660 km divided by 1,485 m/s is 52 minutes.

# PART C — HOURS

## C-01 to C-02 | Vienna processes it

All eleven hydroacoustic stations, plus the seismic and infrasound networks, stream continuously by satellite to the International Data Centre in Vienna.

Software watches every channel for anything unusual. The standard method compares a **short-term average** of the signal against a **long-term average** — recent loudness against background loudness. A sharp ratio change flags a detection. The software then extracts the features listed above: bearing, frequency content, duration, amplitude.

An hour or two later Vienna issues its first automatic bulletin. Human analysts review it and publish a corrected version about two days after that. **Two days is fast for treaty verification and useless for a tsunami.**

---

## C-03 to C-04 | Infrasound

**Infrasound** is sound below about 20 hertz — below the pitch range of human hearing. Large natural events radiate enormous amounts of it, and it travels extraordinarily far, because air absorbs sound in proportion to roughly the square of its frequency. High notes vanish within kilometres. Very low notes cross the planet.

The IMS operates sixty infrasound stations for exactly this reason, as a separate technology from the hydrophones, with its own analysts and bulletins.

Two things worth using:

**People do not hear it, but they perceive it.** Below 20 Hz, loud enough, it registers as pressure in the chest, rattling glass, an unlocatable wrongness. Reliably reported and reliably impossible to describe.

**And where it is heard makes no sense.** Sound bends back down from the stratosphere, so it skips — touching down in rings, missing the ground in between. Large explosions are heard 200 kilometres away and not at 80. **Two Antarctic stations, and the nearer one hears nothing.**

---

## C-05 | The barometric pulse

Below infrasound sits something slower still: a single **pressure pulse** travelling around the world.

The technical name is a **Lamb wave** — a pressure disturbance that hugs the Earth's surface and travels through the lower atmosphere at roughly 300 to 320 metres per second. Not a sound you could hear at any volume. A change in atmospheric pressure, arriving as a step, passing in minutes.

**Any barometer records it.** And a barometer is now a very common object. Every smartphone built since about 2013 contains one, for altitude sensing. Home weather stations have them. Schools have them.

When Hunga Tonga erupted in 2022, the pulse was recorded by **over 1,300 barometers in Japan, eight thousand kilometres away**, as a clean two-hectopascal step. Amateurs across Europe found it in garden weather stations. A school air-monitoring network in Spain picked it out. Somebody in New York wrote a blog post about hearing a volcano with a barometer. Krakatoa in 1883 was caught by roughly fifty barometers worldwide; Hunga Tonga by thousands.

**So: nobody hears your event, and simultaneously a few hundred thousand phones record it.** The dataset exists for days before anyone knows what it is a dataset of.

One symmetry worth having: the Hunga Tonga pulse was detected at **Bharati** — the Indian station in Prydz Bay you destroy in B-06.

---

## C-06 | DART buoys, and the correct wrong answer

A **DART** buoy is two instruments. A pressure sensor sits on the seabed, four kilometres down, measuring the weight of water above it precisely enough to detect a few centimetres of sea level change. A surface buoy relays that to satellite.

They see the first pulse, and what they see says: small, and coming apart.

**Dispersion** is the reason, and it drives your entire plot. When a wave is much longer than the ocean is deep — say two hundred kilometres of wavelength over four kilometres of water — every part of it travels at the same speed, √(gh), and the wave holds together across an ocean. When the wave is *short*, different components travel at different speeds. The long parts outrun the short parts. The packet spreads out and thins into a train of harmless ripples.

Your ice collapse is only about ten kilometres across, so it makes a ten-kilometre wave, and a ten-kilometre wave disperses. **The buoys are measuring this correctly.** The analysis is right, the cancellation is right, and both are right about the wrong thing — because the wave that matters has not been made yet.

Slapping a bathtub versus sweeping your arm through it. Same energy, completely different result.

---

## C-07 to C-10 | The shelves ring each other down

Ice shelves fail in **flexure** — bending. A floating slab riding over long swells flexes up and down, and flexing fatigues ice already close to failure. This is documented: swell from distant storms has been linked to Antarctic calving, and the Tohoku tsunami of 2011 triggered calving at the Sulzberger Ice Shelf, thirteen thousand kilometres away.

**The Southern Ocean is the only ocean that closes on itself.** No continent interrupts it. A long wave leaving any Antarctic embayment travels all the way around at 713 km/h and arrives at every other Antarctic embayment on a schedule you can compute on one sheet of paper.

Prydz to Ross, 6.6 hours. Prydz to Weddell westbound, 7.5. Ross to Amundsen, 4.6.

**The first shelf rings the others down.** No external trigger. No coincidence. One shelf is enough, and the ocean is the messenger.

C-10 is the moment a character computes those three numbers and compares them against the three observed intervals.

---

## C-11 to C-14 | The fans fail, hours later

Here is the part that makes the book work.

Every glaciated continental margin carries a **trough-mouth fan** — a colossal pile of rock flour, ground up by the glacier over millions of years and dumped at the edge of the continental shelf. Thousands of cubic kilometres of fine sediment, resting at the steepest angle it can hold.

Whether it holds depends on **pore pressure** — the water pressure in the spaces between grains. Grains grip each other by friction, and friction depends on how hard they press together. When the water between them carries more of the load, the grains press together less, and the pile loses strength. Wet sand at the beach holds a shape; saturated sand under a breaking wave turns to soup. Same material, different pore pressure.

Change the load on top suddenly and the pore pressure has to redistribute — but water moves through fine mud *slowly*.

**That is your delay, and it costs nothing to justify.** Hours to days. They survive the collapse, they count the icebergs, they think the worst is over.

Then the slope goes, and the difference from the ice is **runout**. Storegga, 8,150 years ago, ran out eight hundred kilometres across the seafloor. A source that translates hundreds of kilometres radiates like a source hundreds of kilometres *wide* — which means long wavelength, which means no dispersion, which means it crosses an ocean.

**Fifteen to thirty minutes of long-period signal, no impulsive onset.** Unmistakably not an earthquake, and unmistakably enormous.

---

## C-15 | The buoys see the real one

Long period, around twenty minutes. Half a metre high in deep water. Not dispersing.

The interval between C-06 and C-15 is your first act.

# PART D — DAYS

## D-01 to D-05 | Arrivals

Deep-water speed is √(gh) — roughly 700 km/h over four kilometres of water. Prydz Bay to the Arabian Sea is about eleven to fifteen hours.

The wave is half a metre high out there and no ship notices. **Shoaling** rebuilds it: as the water shallows the wave slows, and the energy has to go somewhere, so height increases — a factor of four or five coming onto a continental shelf, more in a funnel-shaped bay or harbour.

It does not arrive as a breaking wall. It arrives as a tide that rises for ten minutes and does not stop, then withdraws far enough to strip a harbour to mud, then does it again, for six hours.

**Landslide sources beam.** Energy radiates perpendicular to the direction the sediment moved, like a headlight rather than a bare bulb. One coast is flattened; another, equidistant but off-axis, has a bad day at the marina.

Buenos Aires (D-03) is the worst case in the sequence for a specific reason: the Río de la Plata is 220 km wide at the mouth and 11 metres deep. That is a funnel, and a funnel concentrates.

---

## D-06 | Satellite confirmation

Radar, not cameras — see A-02. In austral winter nothing optical works at all.

---

## D-07 | Antipodal convergence

A wave spreading from a point on a sphere spreads until it passes the equator, then begins converging again, and reconverges at the **antipode** — the point diametrically opposite. Ripples on a pond with no edges; go far enough and you are back at the middle.

**Every Antarctic antipode falls in the Arctic Ocean.** Prydz Bay's is in the Canadian Arctic Archipelago; the Ross Sea's is north of Svalbard.

So the largest wave convergence in recorded history lands on the one ocean with almost no coastline and almost nobody on it. There is no gauge network there and nobody has modelled it. Its practical effect is the fracture of sea ice.

---

## D-08 | The pulse comes round again

The barometric pulse from C-05 circles the Earth. Krakatoa's went round at least three times, passing some barometers seven times. Roughly 35 hours per circuit.

---

## D-09 | Bengal Fan

The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because it is not obvious: **the passing wave is itself a loading event.**

A two-hundred-kilometre wave crossing a continental slope raises and lowers the water column above it by metres. Metres of water is tens of kilopascals of pressure, applied for ten minutes, withdrawn, applied again — for six hours. On a fan already at its angle of repose, that is a loading cycle, and the Bengal Fan is the largest sediment body on Earth.

This is how a slope nine thousand kilometres from Antarctica fails three days after it.

---

## D-10 | The gauges come back

**GLOSS** is the global network of coastal tide gauges — instruments that have measured sea level at harbours for a century or more, mostly to predict tides for shipping.

---

## D-11 | Makran earthquake

A **subduction zone** is where one tectonic plate slides beneath another. They stick, strain accumulates for centuries, and eventually they slip. A **thrust** mechanism means the seafloor jumps upward, which lifts the whole water column above it, which is the classic tsunami generator.

The Makran, off Pakistan and Iran, last ruptured in 1945. The interval since is roughly its expected recurrence. **The segment was late.**

Nothing can prove attribution. What can be stated: ocean tides change crustal stress by one to ten kilopascals and demonstrably change the rate of earthquakes. Your sea level rise at this point is about 0.6 kilopascals — the same kind of nudge, applied everywhere at once, and never taken away.

It cannot *cause* an earthquake. It advances the clock on faults already near failure, and after ten thousand years of loading, many are.

---

## D-12 | Currituck

A mapped, previously-failed slope off Virginia and the Carolinas. Its significance is geographic: until this morning the northern hemisphere had been covering the sequence as somebody else's disaster.

---

## D-13 | Earth rotation

A spinning object's behaviour depends on how its mass is distributed. Move a tenth of a percent of a percent of the Earth's water from one end of the planet to everywhere else and two things change measurably.

**Length of day.** Mass moving away from the axis slows the spin, like a skater extending their arms. Fractions of a millisecond.

**Polar motion.** The axis the Earth spins about wanders slightly relative to the crust. Redistributing mass moves it. This is measured routinely, and ice loss has already been shown to steer it.

The **IERS** publishes both, continuously, as a service to satellite navigation.

**Neither has any consequence whatsoever.** That is the point of the fragment. It is the only single number that expresses the whole event, and it is useless.

---

## D-14 | The hinge of the book

Wave residual decays. Water arrives, sloshes, drains, and the gauge returns to its predicted tide within days.

These do not return. Not at one station out of a hundred and forty-one.

The waves were the part that ended. **Everything before this node is a disaster, which is a shape with an end on it. Everything after is a load.**

# PART E — WEEKS TO MONTHS

## E-01 | Weighing the ice from orbit

**GRACE-FO** is two satellites chasing each other around the Earth about 200 km apart, measuring the distance between themselves with extraordinary precision.

When the leading satellite passes over a concentration of mass, gravity tugs it forward slightly and the gap changes. Measure the gap, map the gravity field, and infer where the mass is. Repeat monthly and you measure where mass has *gone*.

**This is the unarguable number.** Not inferred from ice velocity, not from surface height, not modelled. Mass, weighed directly, from space.

---

## E-02 | The rock springs back immediately

Take weight off a surface and it rises. Two mechanisms, on two clocks.

**Elastic rebound** is instant. The crust behaves like a stiff spring — remove the load and it springs back the same day. Centimetres, and GPS stations on Antarctic bedrock see it immediately.

**Viscous rebound** (F-03) is slow. The mantle beneath the crust is solid rock that flows over long timescales, like extremely stiff honey, and it takes far longer to come back.

Press a finger into a rubber ball and into a jar of honey. Both recover. One recovers now.

---

## E-03 | The ice streams speed up

An **ice stream** is a fast-flowing river of ice within a slower ice sheet — the channels that actually deliver ice to the coast.

Remove the shelf and you remove the cork (see A-01). The streams behind it accelerate.

**This is measured, not theorised.** After Larsen B collapsed in 2002, its tributary glaciers sped up by factors of two to eight within months, and they did not slow down again.

---

## E-04 | Watching the line move

InSAR finds the grounding line by detecting the tidal hinge described in A-04 — the band that flexes twice a day. Repeat over months and you map retreat directly.

---

## E-05 | Is the world shaking more?

Global earthquake catalogues are complete and consistent enough to detect a change in rate. Whether the change is real, and whether it is attributable, becomes an argument that runs for years and never fully resolves. That is realistic and worth keeping.

---

## E-06 to E-07 | The fingerprint

**E-06:** radar altimeters have measured global sea level continuously since 1992. A step of ten to fifteen centimetres is unmissable.

**E-07 is the fact that reframes the politics of your book, and it has been in the literature since the 1970s.**

An ice sheet is a mass, and mass attracts. Antarctica's ice pulls the surrounding ocean toward itself gravitationally, so the sea currently stands *higher* near Antarctica than it otherwise would. Remove the ice and that attraction goes with it. Water flows away.

So sea level near Antarctica **falls**. And the far field receives the global average **plus a further ten to thirty per cent** — with the maximum in the North Atlantic and the North Pacific.

New York, Boston, Rotterdam, Hamburg, London, Shanghai and Tokyo each receive more rise than any southern-hemisphere city that was actually struck by a wave.

**The hemisphere that watched it on television is the hemisphere loaded hardest.**

---

## E-08 to E-10 | Ocean and ice

**E-08:** **Argo** is a fleet of several thousand autonomous floats that drift at depth, rise every ten days measuring temperature and salinity, transmit by satellite, and sink again. They will see the Southern Ocean freshening — a lid of light fresh meltwater sitting on denser salt water below, which suppresses vertical mixing. That matters in F-02.

**E-09:** sea ice responds to the freshening and to the mélange.

**E-10:** the iceberg field disperses into shipping lanes and gets tracked by radar for years.

# PART F — YEARS

## F-01 | The rate becomes the story

Centimetres per year, where the twentieth century managed a few millimetres.

---

## F-02 | The deep ocean engine

Around Antarctica in winter, sea ice forms — and freezing seawater rejects its salt, leaving behind water that is both very cold and very salty, and therefore very dense. It sinks to the bottom and spreads north along the seafloor, filling the deep basins of every ocean. This is **Antarctic Bottom Water**, and it is one of the two engines driving global ocean circulation.

Cap the Southern Ocean with fresh meltwater (E-08) and the surface water is no longer dense enough to sink. **The engine stalls.**

Slow, invisible, enormous, and largely absent from disaster fiction.

---

## F-03 | The slow rebound begins

The viscous half of E-02. And a useful specific: **the mantle under West Antarctica is unusually soft**, so rebound there runs on decades rather than the millennia typical elsewhere. Measured in the Amundsen sector, and fast enough for your characters to watch.

---

## F-04 and G-02 | Why it does not stop

This is the most important idea in Part F, and it is simple.

A glacier's bed normally slopes downward toward the sea, so a retreating grounding line moves into *shallower* water and eventually finds somewhere to stabilise.

Much of West Antarctica has the opposite geometry. The bed slopes downward **going inland** — the basin gets deeper the further from the coast you go.

Now run the retreat. The grounding line moves inland, into deeper water. Deeper water means the ice there is *thicker*. Thicker ice at the boundary discharges *faster*. Faster discharge thins the ice, which pushes the boundary further inland, into water deeper still.

**There is no configuration where it balances.** It runs until the bed rises again — and in the Amundsen sector, the bed does not rise again for about six hundred kilometres.

That is the answer to "when does this stop," and it is why the honest answer to a committee is *we do not have time, we have rate*.

---

## F-05 | Volcanoes

Rock melts under lower pressure. Take a kilometre of ice off the crust and you reduce the pressure on the mantle beneath, and more of it melts. Iceland's eruption rate rose roughly thirtyfold after the last ice age ended.

West Antarctica has an active volcanic rift under the ice.

---

## F-07 and G-01 to G-03 | The long tail

The fingerprint of E-07 reaches full expression. The bulk of three and a half to four and a half metres arrives over decades. The retreat runs toward the reverse-slope end.

There is never a morning when it has happened.

**Antipode** — the point on the far side of the globe. Wave energy reconverges there.
**Argo** — global fleet of profiling floats measuring ocean temperature and salinity.
**Back-azimuth** — the compass bearing a signal arrived from.
**Broadband** — containing many frequencies at once.
**Buttressing** — the back-pressure a floating shelf exerts on the grounded ice behind it. The cork.
**Calving** — an iceberg separating from a glacier or shelf.
**Crevasse** — partial-depth crack. Ordinary.
**DART** — seabed pressure sensor plus surface buoy; detects tsunamis in deep water.
**Dispersion** — a wave packet spreading out because its components travel at different speeds. Kills short waves, spares long ones.
**Flexure** — bending. How swell fatigues an ice shelf.
**Fracture initiation** — the instant a crack starts.
**GLOSS** — the global network of coastal tide gauges.
**GRACE-FO** — paired satellites that weigh mass changes on Earth via their mutual distance.
**Grounding line** — where ice stops resting on rock and starts floating.
**Grounding zone** — the few-kilometre band around it that flexes with the tide.
**Ice shelf** — the floating part. **Ice sheet** — the grounded part. **Ice stream** — a fast river of ice within the sheet.
**Icequake** — a small earthquake in ice.
**Impulsive onset** — a sharp signal start. Earthquakes have one; landslides do not.
**Infrasound** — sound below 20 Hz. Inaudible, travels enormous distances.
**InSAR** — radar interferometry; detects centimetre ground movement between satellite passes.
**Lamb wave** — a pressure pulse hugging the Earth's surface at ~315 m/s. Recorded by barometers.
**Mélange** — jammed floating mass of ice blocks and rubble.
**P wave** — the fastest seismic wave. Arrives first.
**Period** — time for one wave oscillation. Long period = slow = low frequency.
**Pore pressure** — water pressure between sediment grains. Controls whether a slope holds.
**Rayleigh wave** — surface elastic wave; sets the speed limit for a running crack.
**Rift** — full-thickness crack through an ice shelf.
**Runout** — how far a submarine landslide travels. Long runout makes long waves.
**Runup** — how high water climbs the land.
**SAR** — radar imaging. Works through cloud and darkness.
**Shoaling** — a wave slowing and growing taller as water shallows.
**SOFAR channel** — the ocean's sound-speed minimum. Traps sound and carries it across basins.
**Stress** — force per unit area.
**Subduction zone** — where one tectonic plate slides beneath another.
**Thinning** — an ice shelf losing thickness, mostly to melting from below.
**Trough-mouth fan** — a vast pile of glacially-ground sediment at the edge of a continental shelf.
**Unjamming** — a granular solid losing rigidity and beginning to flow.
**√(gh)** — deep-water tsunami speed. Depth in metres, times 9.8, square rooted. About 200 m/s over 4 km.
