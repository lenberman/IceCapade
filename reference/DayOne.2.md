# DayOne.2 — Who listens to the SOFAR channel

Reference note. Companion to DayOne.1. Fields, institutions, and named researchers, with an eye to who is worth contacting for background.

**Standing caveat:** affiliations drift. Check current staff pages before writing to anyone. Points of specific uncertainty are flagged in §4.

## 1. Six communities

They share an instrument and a frequency band, and they mostly do not read each other's journals. That fragmentation is worth using.

### Cryoacoustics / glacial seismology

The field your book lives in. These are the people who study ice by listening to it — icequakes, calving, rifting, iceberg collisions, grounding, stick-slip against the seabed.

Antarctic ice is a known and catalogued source in IMS hydroacoustic data. **Iceberg harmonic tremor** (IHT) is the signature phenomenon: sustained signals lasting up to four hours, fundamental frequency between roughly 1 and 10 Hz, with multiple harmonics that appear as striations on a spectrogram. The mechanism turned out to be long sequences of regularly spaced stick-slip subevents as ice grinds against ice or against the seafloor — not resonance of the iceberg itself, which was the first guess.

IHT has been recorded as T phases on equatorial Pacific islands, as hydroacoustic signals in the Indian Ocean, and by Antarctic seismic networks. **HA01 Cape Leeuwin has its own literature of cryogenic detections.** Popular coverage calls it "singing icebergs."

Directly relevant precedent: the 2016 Nansen Ice Shelf calving, where moored hydrophones recorded hundreds of broadband cryogenic signals across 10–400 Hz for *weeks before* the calving became visible on satellite imagery. The ice announced itself acoustically long before anything showed optically. That is your opening chapter, already published.

### CTBT verification

Vienna, the national data centres, the weapons labs. Small, technical, security-adjacent, and professionally focused on discriminating natural signals from explosions — which means they identify ice correctly and then set it aside.

### Marine bioacoustics

The largest community by headcount. Blue and fin whale calls fall at 15–25 Hz, squarely in the band, so cetacean researchers maintain continuous recorders across the Southern Ocean. **Their archives contain every ice event, earthquake, ship and airgun survey as a by-product.** A bioacoustician may well hold the best independent record of your event without having been looking for it.

### Submarine volcanism and seafloor seismicity

T-phase monitoring of mid-ocean ridge eruptions and ridge-transform earthquakes. Substantial overlap in personnel with cryoacoustics — the same instruments, the same institutions, often the same people.

### Ocean acoustic tomography / acoustic thermometry

Walter Munk's legacy. Sound travel time across a basin depends on temperature, so repeated transmissions measure ocean heat content directly. Historically: the Heard Island Feasibility Test (1991) and ATOC. This community thinks in terms of the channel as a *measuring instrument* rather than a listening post.

### Acoustic-gravity waves for tsunami early warning

Newest, and the one that could break your plot open if you let it.

AGWs travel at near the speed of sound in water — an order of magnitude faster than the tsunami itself — and radiate in all directions regardless of the wave's trajectory. Cardiff's **GREAT** system (Global Real-time Early Alarm for Tsunami) has run on live CTBTO hydroacoustic data since June 2024, accessed via Portugal's IPMA, feeding a Tsunami Warning Centre established at Cardiff. It operates in real time without pre-set templates, and the team has shown that earthquake location, duration, dimensions, orientation and speed can be recovered from a **single hydrophone**.

The acknowledged limitation is the sparseness of the hydroacoustic network — eleven stations.

**Plot consequence:** a university mathematics department now holds a functioning global tsunami detector and no statutory authority whatsoever. If you want someone who understands what happened on Day 0 while the official system files it, this is a more interesting home than any agency.

### (Historical) SOSUS / IUSS

The US Navy's Cold War submarine surveillance network — the reason civilian hydroacoustics exists at the scale it does. Partially opened to scientific use in the early 1990s, which seeded much of the PMEL work. Useful backstory texture for any older character.

Also: **Oskar Glowacki** (Polish Academy of Sciences) on glacier-bay acoustics; **Grant Deane** (Scripps) on ice–ocean acoustic processes; **Andreas Köhler** (NORSAR / University of Oslo) on glacier seismicity.

**Usama Kadri** — Reader in Applied Mathematics, Cardiff University. AGW theory, the GREAT system, and the Cardiff Tsunami Warning Centre. Co-authors include **Ali Abdolali** (NOAA/NCEP background).

### Continuous Antarctic listening

The **Alfred Wegener Institute** operates **PALAOA**, a permanent underwater acoustic observatory on the Ekström Ice Shelf near Neumayer Station. **Ilse van Opzeeland** is the contact. A permanent Antarctic hydrophone with a long continuous record is a strong setting in its own right.

**Ana Širović** (Texas A&M Galveston, formerly Scripps), **Kathleen Stafford** (Oregon State), **Holger Klinck** (Cornell, K. Lisa Yang Center for Conservation Bioacoustics — formerly OSU/PMEL, so fluent in both worlds).

## 5. Practical note on making contact

Cryoacousticians are a small field working on something the public finds charming. "Singing icebergs" gets them press, and they tend to answer novelists.

**Lead with the specific technical question, not the premise.**

> *What would a hundred-kilometre shelf disintegration look like on HA01?*

gets you a real answer, possibly a spectrogram, possibly an hour of someone's time.

> *I'm writing a book where Antarctica collapses*

gets you a polite paragraph about timescales and a link to a review paper.

Two further openings that tend to work: asking what the **largest cryogenic signal on record** looks like, and asking whether anyone has modelled **circumpolar wave propagation between ice shelves** — the second is a real open question and the sort of thing researchers enjoy being asked.

Since 2014, data from the Australian, UK and US stations — four of the six hydrophone arrays — has been openly available. Pulling HA01 yourself before writing to anyone would be a good-faith signal, and would let you ask a sharper question.
